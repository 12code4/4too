# The Wrong Axis: Verbs, Computational Form, and the Under-Explored Design Spaces of the Videogame

*Abstract — The commercial vocabulary that sorts videogames — "shooter," "role-playing game," "platformer" — classifies them by their resemblance to older media rather than by what they let a player do. Following Apperley (2006), we argue that these marketing genres measure the wrong axis and "obscure the new medium's crucial defining feature," interactivity. We reconstruct an alternative vocabulary in which a design space is a possibility space fixed by the player's verbs (Anthropy & Clark, 2014; Salen & Zimmerman, 2004; Upton, 2015), by subject matter, and by the computational form unique to the medium (Murray, 1997; Bogost, 2007; Romero, 2009). Along that axis the commercial medium is revealed to cluster tightly around two verbs, violence and acquisition — a monoculture that content analysis (Smith, Lachlan & Tamborini, 2003), Koster's (2004) mastery theory of fun, and platform-moderation policy (Yang, 2017) each independently predict. Surveying seven under-explored spaces through their pioneer titles, we argue the frontier stayed closed for economic and legal reasons, not for want of imagination — a diagnosis we read as hopeful.*

## Introduction

The way an industry names its products quietly decides what it will build. Walk any digital storefront and the shelves are labelled "shooter," "role-playing game," "platformer," "fighting game," "real-time strategy." These labels feel native to the medium, as though a videogame simply *is* one of these categories the way a mammal is one of its orders. They are not native. They are a retail taxonomy inherited from film, pulp fiction, and toy marketing, and they sort games chiefly by how much a game resembles something that came before — its camera, its fiction, its theme. This paper argues three things in sequence. First, that this taxonomy measures the wrong axis, and that the right axis is the player's *verb* together with the *computational form* only this medium can offer. Second, that when the medium is re-sorted along that axis, the commercial videogame is revealed to have explored an astonishingly narrow band of the possible, clustering around two verbs — to destroy and to accumulate — while entire design spaces sit almost empty, each marked by one or two extraordinary pioneer titles and nearly no followers. Third, and most consequentially for a studio deciding where to invest, that these spaces stayed empty for reasons that are economic, technical, and even legal rather than imaginative.

That last claim matters because the two diagnoses point in opposite directions. If the frontier is empty because players do not want what lies beyond it, a studio should stay home. If it is empty because the frontier is built from the two most expensive substances in game development — hand-authored reactivity and deep simulation — then the barrier is a budget and a toolchain, and budgets and toolchains are exactly the kind of constraint that technology dissolves over time. The evidence assembled here, drawn entirely from the design-theory and pioneer-game literatures, supports the second diagnosis. A companion paper builds on this economic argument directly; the present paper's task is to establish the map and the mechanism.

## Background and Related Work

Two research traditions converge on the claim that marketing genre is a poor description of what a game is. The first is classificatory. Apperley (2006), writing in *Simulation & Gaming*, argues that "market-based categories of genre obscure the new medium's crucial defining feature by dividing [games] into categories loosely organized by similarities to prior forms of mediation." Conventional genres, he holds, "rely overmuch on games' representational characteristics" — the look and theme borrowed from cinema and fiction — and he proposes instead a vocabulary built on "nonrepresentational, specifically interactive characteristics." Clarke, Lee, and Clark (2017), approaching from library and information science, reach a compatible conclusion by a different route: genre in games serves identity, retrieval, commercial marketing, and instruction, and these purposes actively pull against coherent classification. The same title routinely lands in different genres across storefronts, and hybrid labels such as "Action-RPG" and "Action-adventure" quietly concede that the scheme has broken; they recommend faceted classification, family resemblance, and community tags in its place. The lesson is not that genre is useless but that it is a *retail* instrument mistaken for an *analytic* one.

The second tradition supplies the analytic vocabulary the first one lacks. Salen and Zimmerman (2004) define good design as the production of *meaningful play*, which "gives players expansive spaces of possibility — sets of choices and outcomes that represent interesting possible ways to play." Upton (2015) compresses the idea to a thesis: "play is free movement within a system of constraints," where each constraint must "serve a specific function in structuring the overall experience." Anthropy and Clark (2014) locate the atom of that system in the *verb* — the actions a player can take, "jump," "shoot," "fall," "flap" — which they define as "the rules that allow the player to interact with the other rules," inheriting Church's (1999) earlier call for a shared vocabulary with which to "dissect a game into its components." Running parallel is a lineage that locates the medium's expressive capacity not in its verbs alone but in its *computational form*: Murray (1997) names the digital medium's four affordances — procedural, participatory, encyclopedic, spatial — and singles out the procedural, the capacity to embody and execute rules, as the defining new expressive property; Bogost (2007) coins "procedural rhetoric," "the art of persuasion through rule-based representations and interactions, rather than the spoken word, writing, images, or moving pictures." Juul's (2005) *Half-Real* grounds the whole edifice: we play by real, binding rules while imagining an optional fiction, so the rules, not the fiction, are the load-bearing structure. This paper stands on that convergence and asks a question the theorists mostly left implicit: if verbs and computational form are the real axes, what does the medium's actual distribution along them look like?

## Verbs, Subject Matter, and Form: What a Design Space Actually Is

A *design space*, on the account assembled above, is a possibility space fixed by three coordinates, none of which appears on a storefront label. The first is the set of core **verbs** — what the player actually does, moment to moment, as an act of agency inside the system. The second is **subject matter** — what those verbs are *about*, the human situation the game asks the player to inhabit. The third is the **computational or rule form** through which the first two are expressed, the property Murray (1997) and Bogost (2007) insist is unique to the medium and unavailable to any older art. The decisive test of whether meaning lives in this triple rather than in the audiovisual surface is Brenda Romero's *Train* (2009), one of the analog works in her series "The Mechanic Is the Message." *Train* has no graphics at all. Players load little yellow pegs, standing for people, efficiently into boxcars; the game's meaning is carried by the loading *verb* and by material components that encode subject matter procedurally — a vintage Nazi typewriter that prints the game's rules, the board resting on a pane of broken glass evoking Kristallnacht, and a face-down destination card that finally reads *Auschwitz* (Romero, 2009). If a graphics-free board game can be a canonical example of expressive design, then graphics cannot be the frontier, and the marketing genres — which are almost entirely descriptions of graphical and fictional surface — cannot be describing the thing that matters.

Two clarifying tools follow. Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek's (2004) MDA framework separates Mechanics from Dynamics from Aesthetics and observes that designers build from mechanics toward aesthetics while players experience the reverse, which is exactly why a surface-first taxonomy misleads: it names the aesthetic wrapper and ignores the mechanical engine. Hocking's (2007) diagnosis of *BioShock* — that its harvesting, self-interested *gameplay* contract contradicts its anti-Objectivist *narrative* contract — only makes sense once one accepts that a game's real argument is carried by its verbs and not its script; tellingly, the term he coined, "ludonarrative dissonance," appears only in his essay's title and never in its body, and has since drifted far from that precise meaning. The concept is worth reclaiming, because it is the sharpest available proof that verbs, not fiction, do a game's arguing.

## The Two-Verb Monoculture: Violence, Acquisition, and Why They Won

Re-sorted along the verb axis, the commercial medium looks not diverse but startlingly monocultural. Its dominant verbs are to *destroy* and to *acquire* — to shoot, strike, and detonate, and to loot, level, and accumulate. The empirical anchor is content analysis: Smith, Lachlan, and Tamborini (2003), coding sixty of the most popular games across three consoles with a scheme adapted from the National Television Violence Study, found violence in the majority of popular titles, with a summary figure near sixty-eight percent, and industry-level statements attributed to the American Psychological Association put "some form of violence" at roughly eighty-five percent of games on the market. The exact proportion varies by sample and definition, but the direction is not in doubt. What makes the finding more than a talking point is that three independent explanations — one psychological, one economic, one infrastructural — each predict it, and they do not depend on one another.

The psychological explanation is Koster's (2004). Fun, he argues, "arises out of mastery ... out of comprehension ... the act of solving puzzles," the brain being "a giant pattern-recognizing machine." This yields a non-obvious corollary the theory itself never states. Combat supplies crisp, quantifiable, masterable feedback — hit or miss, hit points, win or lose — so it is *cheap to make fun* under Koster's model. The under-served verbs of the human repertoire — to mourn, to tend, to negotiate, to translate, to repair — produce diffuse, ambiguous, socially contingent feedback that resists the same legibility, and are therefore *structurally harder to render fun*. That the argument comes from Koster, a designer of the commercial mainstream who explicitly frames this bias toward "top-monkey" survival lessons as a limitation the medium should evolve past, is what makes it damning rather than merely descriptive. The economic explanation follows from the classificatory tradition already reviewed: capital concentrates in the proven marketing categories, and those categories are overwhelmingly the violent ones.

The infrastructural explanation is the most concrete, and it is this paper's sharpest single piece of evidence that the under-served verbs are *suppressed* rather than merely unpopular. For *The Tearoom* (2017), Robert Yang replaced the explicit male genitalia in his game with firearms, in order to evade the moderation systems of Twitch and YouTube, writing that he had swapped them for "the only thing that the game industry will never moderate nor ban — guns" (Yang, 2017). The observation operates at the level of platform policy: the shared infrastructure that gates commercial games treats violence as the unmarked, always-permitted default and intimacy as the transgressive exception. A penis is bannable; a gun never is. The "shoot" default is thus not a matter of taste but a property of the pipes through which games reach players. To the two dominant verbs we should add acquisition as their quieter twin — the loot table, the experience bar, the ever-expanding inventory — for the same underlying reason Koster identifies: accumulation is legible, quantifiable, and endlessly masterable, and, as the next sections argue, it is also nearly free to produce.

## Mapping the Empty Frontier

If violence and acquisition occupy the crowded center, what lies in the empty regions? The pioneer-game record identifies at least seven design spaces that are demonstrably *real* — each opened by a title that works, that critics honored, and in several cases that sold — yet almost *empty*, followed by a handful of imitators at most. Table 1 maps each space to its pioneers and to the verb or formal property it exploits.

**Table 1. Seven under-explored design spaces, their pioneers, and the verb or computational form each exploits.**

| Under-explored space | Pioneer title(s) | Verb / formal property exploited |
|---|---|---|
| Decipherment / language-as-play | *Heaven's Vault* (inkle, 2019); *Chants of Sennaar* (Rundisc, 2023); *Tunic* (Shouldice, 2022) | **translate** — the player's own growing literacy is the mechanic |
| Epistemic deduction | *Return of the Obra Dinn* (Pope, 2018); *Outer Wilds* (Mobius Digital, 2019) | **know** — knowledge is the sole axis of progression |
| Social negotiation | *Disco Elysium* (ZA/UM, 2019); *Façade* (Mateas & Stern, 2005); *Versu* (Short & Evans, 2013) | **converse** — dialogue is the resolving system; combat absent |
| Care / stewardship / anti-4X | *Papers, Please* (Pope, 2013); *Terra Nil* (Free Lives, 2023); *Spiritfarer* (Thunder Lotus, 2020) | **tend / restore / release** — accumulation reversed |
| Living-world "story generator" | *Dwarf Fortress* (Bay 12, 2022); *RimWorld* (Sylvester, 2018); Nemesis system (Monolith, 2014) | **simulate** — narrative emerges from deep systems |
| Impossible space | *Antichamber* (Bruce, 2013); *Manifold Garden* (Chyr, 2019); *Superliminal* (Pillow Castle, 2019) | **traverse** — non-Euclidean and relativistic geometry as puzzle |
| Time as material | *The Longing* (Studio Seufz, 2020) | **wait** — real elapsed time is the core resource |

Consider decipherment first. *Heaven's Vault* (inkle, 2019) built an entire logographic "Ancient" language — some forty-six glyphs and over a thousand words of translatable inscription — whose verb is the player's own act of translation. Writer-director Jon Ingold framed the goal as wanting "the player to feel like they're translating something," designing a language logical enough to learn yet poetic enough to surprise: the word for *God* is "Person of knowledge," and the word for *value* is the same as the word for *water*; inkle's Joseph Humfrey called the result "the Guitar Hero of linguistics" (inkle, 2019). The commercial ceiling is legible in the numbers — a third-party estimate puts the flagship's Steam gross near $390,000 — which is part of why the space had so few followers until *Chants of Sennaar* (Rundisc, 2023), a Toulouse studio's Tower-of-Babel decipherment game, swept France's national Pégases awards in 2024, taking Best Game, Best Independent Game, and Best Game Design, adding Best Indie at the New York Game Awards and a place on the *New York Times* top-ten of 2023 (CNC, 2024; Totilo, 2023). *Tunic* (Shouldice, 2022), whose constructed "Trunic" script fills an in-game manual most players never fully read, extends the same verb; Shouldice built "a strange, unreadable language" precisely to make the player "feel like they were in a world that wasn't meant for them" (Shouldice, 2022). The lineage reaches back to *Sethian* (Kuning, 2016), a solo-built logographic-language puzzle whose "Sethianese" borrows from Chinese and American Sign Language.

The epistemic space is defined by a still purer constraint: knowledge is the *only* form of progression. In *Return of the Obra Dinn* (Pope, 2018), the player reconstructs the fates of all sixty souls aboard a ghost ship, the game confirming deductions only in batches of three so that guessing is punished; its 1-bit dithered rendering, evoking a Macintosh Plus, is itself an engineering feat, and it took the IGF 2019 Grand Prize and BAFTA awards for Game Design and Artistic Achievement (Pope, 2018; GDC, 2019). *Outer Wilds* (Mobius Digital, 2019) sets a twenty-two-minute solar-system time loop in which nothing is upgraded and nothing is grinded; a player who *knows* the answer can finish on the first loop, because, as director Alex Beachum puts it, knowledge is "the only real thing of value in this world" (Beachum, 2019). It won the 2020 BAFTA for Best Game. Around these anchors sit *The Case of the Golden Idol* (2022), *Her Story* (Barlow, 2015) — whose creator became the first solo developer to win three BAFTAs in a single year — and *Immortality* (Barlow, 2022) with its match-cut mechanic. Notably, this is the one surveyed space visibly *filling in*: *The Roottrees Are Dead* (Ward, 2023/2025) and *Lorelei and the Laser Eyes* (Simogo, 2024) show the epistemic frontier growing through 2024–2026.

Three further spaces can be treated together because they share a single scarce ingredient. Social negotiation replaces combat with conversation: *Disco Elysium* (ZA/UM, 2019) carries roughly a million words of branching dialogue across twenty-four skills that speak to the player as internal voices, resolves everything through skill checks rather than fighting, and, corrected against a widely repeated error, sold on the order of 2.6 million copies rather than the "5 million" often cited (LEVVVEL, n.d.; PSU, 2021); it took four wins at The Game Awards 2019 (Kerr, 2019). Its cautionary shadow is *Façade* (Mateas & Stern, 2005), a natural-language interactive drama that consumed roughly five self-funded years and became famous for its brittleness under free text (Mateas & Stern, 2005), and *Versu* (Short & Evans, 2013), a social-agent dialogue platform that Linden Lab simply switched off — a reminder that a pioneering *toolchain* can vanish, and that conversation remains, in Emily Short's phrase, the most theorized yet least productized mechanic in the medium (Short, 2019). Care and stewardship invert the accumulation verb: *Papers, Please* (Pope, 2013) makes bureaucratic document-checking the mechanic and won not only IGF and BAFTA honors but a Peabody Award for Digital and Interactive Storytelling (Papers Please Wiki, n.d.); *Terra Nil* (Free Lives, 2023) is an explicit "reverse city-builder" in which you restore a wasteland and then recycle your machinery and leave no trace (Game World Observer, 2022); *Spiritfarer* (Thunder Lotus, 2020) advertises itself as "a cozy management game about dying," where the mechanic *is* saying goodbye (Thunder Lotus, 2020). Living-world simulation treats the systemic engine itself as the product: *Dwarf Fortress* (Bay 12, 2022), whose motto is "Losing is Fun," sold 160,000 copies in its first 24 hours on Steam against a two-month projection of the same number and passed a million by 2025 (Game Developer, 2025; Game World Observer, 2022); *RimWorld* (Sylvester, 2018) is designed "as a story generator, not a game," paced by three named AI Storytellers, on the principle that "loss is an essential part of a story, not its conclusion" (Sylvester, 2017).

The final two spaces are the most literal about form. Impossible space makes geometry the puzzle: *Antichamber* (Bruce, 2013) builds non-Euclidean corridors that change when unobserved; *Manifold Garden* (Chyr, 2019) folds infinite Escher geometry with multi-directional gravity; *Superliminal* (Pillow Castle, 2019) rescales objects to their apparent size. Time-as-material makes duration itself the resource: *The Longing* (Studio Seufz, 2020) asks the player to wait four hundred real days for a sleeping king to wake, its clock advancing even when the game is closed — a design so anti-viral it cannot meaningfully be demonstrated. Seven spaces, and in most of them the pioneers can be counted on one hand.

## The Economics of the Closed Frontier

Why do these spaces stay empty when their pioneers win the medium's highest honors and, in several cases, sell in the millions? The answer is not that players reject them; it is that the spaces are built from the two most expensive substances in game development, and are fenced by a cluster of specific barriers the pioneer record documents. The first expensive substance is **hand-authored reactivity** — bespoke text and events written to respond to particular player states. Its defining property, in a phrase this paper offers as its own analysis, is that reactive content *does not tile*: you cannot lay down one authored beat and repeat it across the map the way you repeat a texture or a combat encounter, because its whole value is its specificity to a moment. *Disco Elysium*'s million words and *Heaven's Vault*'s entire constructed language are reactivity at industrial scale; *Façade*'s five self-funded years are the same cost paid in AI-authoring time. The second expensive substance is **deep systems** — the simulation depth behind *Dwarf Fortress* and *RimWorld* — which resists the modular reuse that shipping schedules depend on.

On top of these substances sit four documented barriers. The **one-shot problem** afflicts decipherment and deduction directly: knowledge, once known, cannot be un-known, so the core loop is spent the moment the player succeeds, capping replay value and complicating word-of-mouth (the flagship's ~$390,000 Steam gross concretizes the ceiling). **Authoring cost** is astronomical and has already been quantified above. **Development time** is long and artisanal across the whole frontier: *Tunic*, *Antichamber*, and *Manifold Garden* each took roughly seven years, *Superliminal* about six, *Caves of Qud* fifteen (PC Gamer, 2024), and *Dwarf Fortress* sixteen before its Steam release — the labor itself is a barrier to entry. And most striking, a design space can be **legally enclosed**. Warner Bros. patented the Nemesis system of *Middle-earth: Shadow of Mordor* (Monolith, 2014) — the procedural enemies who remember encounters and nurse vendettas — with a patent that does not expire until 11 August 2036, and then shut down Monolith in 2025 (GameSpot, n.d.; TechRadar, n.d.). The studio that invented the mechanic is gone, while the patent goes on chilling the entire space around it.

Set against this, the economics of the two dominant verbs are almost embarrassingly favorable. A combat system is authored once and then *rearranged* endlessly — new enemy stats, new arenas, new weapons draped over the identical underlying loop — so its cost amortizes across an entire game and, via engines and middleware, across the entire industry. Loot is better still: procedurally generated, it is effectively infinite at nearly zero marginal authoring cost, which is precisely why the acquisition verb scales without limit. Violence and acquisition did not win because they are the deepest veins of human meaning. They won because they *tile*. This is the paper's central economic claim, and it reframes the emptiness of the frontier: the spaces beyond the center are not commercially impossible, they are commercially *expensive*, and expense is a moving target.

## Discussion

Several findings in the preceding argument are non-obvious enough to restate, because each overturns a comfortable assumption about why the medium looks as it does. The first is that the medium's default verb is provable at the level of platform policy rather than merely inferred from sales charts. Yang's (2017) substitution of guns for genitals in *The Tearoom* — because guns are "the only thing that the game industry will never moderate nor ban" — turns the abstract claim "combat dominates" into a documented fact about how Twitch, YouTube, and console certification encode violence as the permitted default and intimacy as transgression. The suppression is infrastructural, not attitudinal.

The second is that the academy named marketing genre as the wrong axis two decades ago, and named it precisely: Apperley's (2006) charge that genres "obscure the new medium's crucial defining feature" is not a rhetorical flourish but a classificatory claim, corroborated independently by Clarke, Lee, and Clark (2017) from information science. Genre feels native to games; it is in fact a borrowed retail taxonomy. The third is that Koster's (2004) mastery theory of fun *secretly predicts* the violence monoculture: the dominance of combat is not only ideological but falls straight out of "fun equals mastery of legible patterns," which starves the ambiguous verbs of investment because they are genuinely harder to make legible — a diagnosis delivered, pointedly, from inside the commercial mainstream. The fourth is that a design space can be fenced by patent law until 2036, a barrier with no analogue in older media, where no one owns the mechanic of the flashback or the unreliable narrator.

The fifth finding complicates the very frontier this paper celebrates. The care-and-stewardship space is not the uncontested feel-good territory it appears: peer-reviewed game-studies scholarship now reads cozy games as *both* an escape from and an "indoctrination into" neoliberal productivity ideology, theorizing the cozy as a "fantasy of stability" rather than a "fantasy of greatness" and drawing on Giddens' notion of ontological security (Replay, 2024). A studio entering this space should know it is entering a live critical argument, not a safe harbor. The sixth is a quiet dignity marker: real philologists take decipherment games seriously. The University of Cambridge's CREWS Project, an ERC-funded ancient-writing-systems programme, published analysis of *Heaven's Vault* and *Sethian* as genuine decipherment artefacts (CREWS Project, 2019). A design space can be simultaneously commercially marginal and academically substantive.

The through-line binding these findings is the economic one. The frontier is gated by content-and-systems cost, and every barrier surveyed — authoring, one-shot exhaustion, artisanal development time, even legal enclosure — is a variant of "this is too expensive to make and too hard to reuse." That is a hopeful diagnosis, not a pessimistic one. A frontier closed because audiences are absent stays closed. A frontier closed because reactive content and deep systems are expensive to author is a frontier that procedural tooling, better authoring pipelines, and falling production costs can progressively open. The visible in-filling of the epistemic-deduction space through 2024–2026 — *Golden Idol*, *Roottrees*, *Lorelei* arriving in a steady cadence behind *Obra Dinn* and *Outer Wilds* — is exactly what the beginning of such an opening looks like.

### Limitations

This analysis inherits the limits of its evidence. The content-analytic anchor (Smith, Lachlan & Tamborini, 2003) is now two console generations old, and its "majority-violent" figure should be read as directional rather than current; a contemporary replication might shift the exact proportion even if the pattern holds. Several commercial figures — *Heaven's Vault*'s Steam gross, *Disco Elysium*'s ~2.6 million copies — are third-party tracker estimates, not audited publisher numbers, and are cited as such. The seven-space taxonomy is illustrative, not exhaustive: it foregrounds spaces with clear pioneers and omits borderline cases, and the boundaries between spaces are porous (*Disco Elysium* is as much an epistemic game as a conversational one). Finally, "under-explored" is a claim about relative density that this paper argues qualitatively through pioneer scarcity rather than measuring against a complete census of released titles; a fully quantitative map of the medium's verb distribution remains future work.

## Conclusion

The videogame industry sorts its output by resemblance to older media, and in doing so it has mistaken a retail taxonomy for a description of its own art. Re-sorted along the axis the medium actually runs on — the player's verbs, the subject matter those verbs engage, and the computational form unique to the medium — the commercial catalogue is revealed as a monoculture of two verbs, destruction and acquisition, that content analysis documents, Koster's theory of fun predicts, and platform policy enforces. Beyond that crowded center lie at least seven real but nearly empty design spaces, each proven by pioneers that won the medium's highest honors: decipherment, epistemic deduction, social negotiation, care and stewardship, living-world simulation, impossible space, and time as material. They stayed empty not because players spurned them but because they are built from hand-authored reactivity and deep systems — content that does not tile — and fenced by one-shot exhaustion, artisanal development times, and, in one case, a patent good until 2036. Violence and acquisition won the medium because they are cheap and infinitely reusable, not because they are its deepest subjects. That is the hopeful reading a studio should take from this map: the frontier is gated by cost, and cost is the kind of constraint technology exists to dissolve.
