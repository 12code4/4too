# Content That Does Not Tile: The Under-Explored Game Frontier and a Language Model's Comparative Advantage as a Single Axis

*Abstract — The two prior papers in this set reach the same boundary from opposite sides. The first argues that the videogame's most important unexplored design spaces — decipherment, social negotiation, epistemic deduction, living-world simulation — stayed empty not because players spurned them but because they are built from hand-authored reactivity and deep systems, "content that does not tile." The second argues that a large language model's comparative advantage as a programmer is precisely the collapse of that cost: it is strong on code, rules, text, and verifiable generated content and useless at bespoke art and hand-tuned feel. This synthesis argues the two claims are one claim. The frontier and the tool are the same shape because they are the same fact — a game's material composition — read once by the economics of hand-authoring and once by the economics of machine generation. We ground the overlap space by space (Heaven's Vault's 46 glyphs; Generative Agents at TrueSkill μ=29.89 over a human μ=22.95; CICERO's top-decile Diplomacy), propose three concepts that live inside it, and confront the honest tension: unbounded generation homogenizes and fails, so the win condition is not "the model writes the game" but a mixed-initiative cage of authored constraint around machine-filled volume.*

## Introduction

The recurring promise that a language model will soon "make games" is, as the second paper in this set insists, too coarse to be useful — a videogame is not one artifact but a stack of very different ones, and a model's competence across that stack is wildly uneven. Yet dismissing the coarse claim leaves a sharper one standing. Two companion papers, written independently and pointed at different questions, turn out to describe a single territory from opposite sides. The first mapped the videogame's under-explored design spaces and asked why they stayed empty; the second mapped a language model's programming competence and asked which games it could genuinely build. Laid over one another, the two maps register — not loosely, but almost line for line. The region the first identifies as the medium's most important unexplored frontier is the region the second identifies as the model's sharpest comparative advantage, and the regions each marks as *off* the frontier and *outside* the advantage coincide just as exactly. The claim of this paper is that the registration is not a coincidence but a single fact about what these games are made of, seen twice.

The synthesis lives in the overlap of two economic diagnoses, so both must be stated precisely. Paper 1's diagnosis is that the commercial medium clusters around two verbs, destruction and acquisition, because those verbs *tile*: a combat system is authored once and rearranged endlessly across new enemies and arenas, and loot is procedurally generated at near-zero marginal cost, so both amortize across a whole game and, through engines and middleware, across the whole industry (Anthropy & Clark, 2014; Koster, 2004). The under-explored spaces, by contrast, are built from the two most expensive substances in game development — hand-authored reactivity and deep simulation — and reactive content, by its nature, does not tile. Its entire value is its specificity to a moment; you cannot lay one authored beat across the map the way you repeat a texture. Paper 2's diagnosis is the mirror image, and it too is economic: a language model collapses the cost of exactly one axis — code, rules, text, and verifiable generated content — while leaving the art-audio-feel axis precisely as expensive as it was. Its closing sentence is the hinge this paper turns on: the model "collapse[s] the cost of the code-rules-text-content axis while leaving the art-audio-feel axis exactly as expensive as it was."

Set the two sentences side by side and the argument nearly writes itself. The substance that gates the frontier — reactivity and systems, which is to say code, rules, and text — is the substance whose cost the model collapses; the substance the model cannot touch — bespoke art and hand-tuned feel — is the substance the frontier is mostly not made of. Both alignments hold at once, for one reason: a game's material composition is a single property, read twice, once by the economics of paying humans to author it and once by the economics of asking a machine to generate it. The frontier was closed because that material was expensive to author; the tool is powerful because that same material is cheap to generate. In a quite literal sense, the frontier and the tool were waiting for each other.

## Background and Related Work

Three research literatures inform this argument, and they rarely speak to one another. The first is the design-theory tradition that supplies the frontier's map. Apperley (2006) charged that marketing genres "obscure the new medium's crucial defining feature" by sorting games according to their resemblance to older media rather than to what a player does; the correction, developed by Anthropy and Clark (2014), Salen and Zimmerman (2004), and Upton (2015), relocates the atom of design in the player's *verb* and defines good design as the production of "meaningful play," "free movement within a system of constraints." Running parallel is the claim that the medium's expressive capacity lives in its *computational form* — Murray's (1997) procedural affordance, Bogost's (2007) "procedural rhetoric" — the property that a game's argument is carried by its rules, not its fiction. Along that axis the commercial catalogue looks monocultural, and at least seven design spaces sit almost empty behind lonely pioneers.

The second literature is the benchmarking of code-generating models, which over five years has grown precise enough to draw a model's competence as a shape rather than a score. Function synthesis is saturated — HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) sit at 96–98% pass@1 — but the shape falls away along three axes the successor benchmarks isolate: horizon (METR, 2025), novelty, and grounding. LiveCodeBench Pro (Zheng et al., 2025), annotated by Olympiad medalists, records "catastrophic degradation" on observation-heavy categories pointedly named "game theory" and "interactive," where the best model scored 0% on the hard tier and produced "confidently incorrect justifications" that expert humans do not; grounding fails too, as Spracklen et al. (2025) found 19.7% of referenced packages simply do not exist. Against that sits one consistent positive result: every robust generative result in the procedural-content literature is a generator wrapped in a deterministic verifier — MarioGPT's A\* completability check (Sudhakaran et al., 2023), GAVEL's Ludii grammar (Todd et al., 2024), AlphaCode's execution filter over millions of candidates (Li et al., 2022), TransCoder-ST's generated unit tests (Roziere et al., 2021), all descending from the PCGML survey (Summerville et al., 2018). The model's reliability is not uniform intelligence but *checkable* output.

The third literature is the newest and connects the other two: LLM-driven agents and generative content in games. Park et al. (2023) wrapped a language model in a memory stream, retrieval, reflection, and planning to produce twenty-five "generative agents" whose emergent social behavior human raters judged *more believable than that of human crowdworkers*, and CICERO reached human-level Diplomacy by grounding a dialogue model in a planning-and-reinforcement engine (Meta FAIR, 2022). But the same literature documents how unbounded generation fails — AI Dungeon's coherence collapse and safety catastrophe (Latitude, 2019; Utah Business, 2021) — and why the field's consensus is mixed-initiative: authored constraint around machine volume, from Tanagra's guaranteed-playable levels (Smith, Whitehead & Mateas, 2010) to Compton and Mateas's (2015) "casual creators." This paper stands where the three meet and argues they were describing one thing.

## The Shared Axis: One Fact Read Twice

The strongest way to see that the frontier and the tool are the same shape is to notice that a game's material composition determines two independent things at once, and that nobody designed it to. The first is whether the game was economical to hand-author. A game made largely of bespoke reactive text and deep systems is ruinously expensive, because those substances do not tile — every reactive beat is written once and consumed once, and simulation depth resists the modular reuse shipping schedules depend on. A game made largely of a repeatable combat loop and procedural loot is cheap, because those substances amortize without limit. That is Paper 1's economic axis, and it explains the monoculture: violence and acquisition won the medium not because they are its deepest subjects but because they are its cheapest, most reusable ones.

The second thing that composition determines is whether a language model can produce the game at all. A game made of code, rules, and text is favorable to the model, because that material is the high-volume, low-novelty, checkable output it generates reliably. A game made of bespoke 3D art, recorded performance, and playtested "feel" defeats it, because that material has no cheap oracle — its only judge is human kinesthetic taste, the "juice" of Swink (2008) and the thirty stacked embellishments of Nijman (2013), discovered in the hand and unavailable to a specification. That is Paper 2's capability axis.

Here is the point on which the whole synthesis rests: these are not two axes but one axis read from two directions. "Made of reactive text and systems rather than art and feel" is a single property of a game, and it is exactly the property that made the frontier expensive to author *and* the property that makes it cheap for the model to generate. The correlation is neither spooky nor lucky — cost-to-author and cost-to-generate are both functions of the same underlying variable, what the content physically consists of, so they must move together. The frontier is a coherent set precisely because "expensive for humans" and "cheap for machines" are the same condition wearing different clothes.

The exclusions confirm the alignment better than the inclusions do. The model's weak zone is bespoke art and hand-tuned feel judged by kinesthetic sensation — and that is the one part of the frontier where the frontier mostly is *not*. Of Paper 1's seven spaces, five (decipherment, epistemic deduction, social negotiation, care and stewardship, living-world simulation) are made of text, rules, and systems, squarely in the model's advantage. The two exceptions are the two most kinesthetic — impossible space, whose verb is *traverse* through non-Euclidean geometry, and time-as-material, whose verb is *wait* — and they are precisely the two where a language model would help least, because their value is felt, not written. That the alignment holds even at its own boundary, the frontier's feel-based edges matching the model's feel-based blind spot, is the sharpest evidence that we are looking at one fact and not a happy overlap of two.

## Reactivity, Not Branching: The Cost Curve the Model Attacks

The economic argument becomes concrete when it is priced, and the reactive-narrative RPG prices it more clearly than any other genre. Consider the twenty-five-year cost curve of the form. *Planescape: Torment* (Black Isle, 1999) carried roughly 800,000 words of dialogue — more than a million counting all text — one of the most text-heavy games of its era (Codex Gamicus, n.d.). *Disco Elysium* (ZA/UM, 2019) reached beyond a million words, resolving everything through skill checks rather than combat, and its Final Cut spent about fourteen months voicing that script, the narrator alone recording some 350,000 words (DualShockers, 2021; PSU, 2021). *Baldur's Gate 3* (Larian, 2023) roughly doubled the landmark again to about two million words, with some 174 hours of cinematics (Tech4Gamers, 2023). Reactive narrative depth has doubled per flagship title while remaining, at every step, entirely hand-authored. That is the economic pressure the entire "AI content in games" field exists to relieve.

The number most often misread is *Baldur's Gate 3*'s "17,000 endings," and correcting the misreading is what turns the cost curve into an argument. Larian's lead writer Adam Smith clarified that the figure describes roughly 17,000 *permutations* of a convergent narrative — a "spiderweb" that pulls toward a central endpoint — not 17,000 distinct branches (WccfTech, 2023). This distinction is the crux. The two million words are not spent on branching, on carving the story into thousands of separate paths. They are spent on *reactivity*: on the world noticing what the player did and answering it — a line acknowledging a dead companion, a merchant who remembers an earlier slight, a scene that plays differently because of a choice made ten hours ago. Reactivity, not branching, is what costs words, because each reactive beat is bespoke to a state the player might reach and is worthless in any other. This is "content that does not tile," priced to the syllable.

And it is exactly the cost a language model attacks. The model's native material, per Paper 2, is high-volume, low-novelty text whose correctness is checkable against a world-model's state — "hundreds of consistent room descriptions, synonym tables, and response variants," each verifiable against what the game knows to be true. Reactivity is that shape almost by definition, so the two-million-word explosion is less an argument for the model writing *Baldur's Gate 3* than a map of where hand-authoring runs out of budget, drawn on precisely the axis the model makes cheap. The frontier's most expensive region and the model's most productive one are, again, the same coordinates.

## The Overlap, Space by Space

The abstract claim is best tested by walking the overlap concretely, and four of the frontier's spaces sit inside it cleanly enough to serve as proof. The table below states each as the single fact seen twice: the substance that gated it, the capability the model now supplies, and the oracle or authored cage that makes the capability trustworthy.

| Frontier space (Paper 1) | The substance that does not tile | What the model supplies (Paper 2) | Oracle / authored cage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decipherment | A whole constructed language, hand-built once (*Heaven's Vault*: 46 glyphs, ~3,000 words) | Constructs the conlang itself — phonology, morphology, script — as symbol manipulation over a closed system | Grammar / internal-consistency check; regeneration per playthrough |
| Social negotiation | Millions of words of dialogue plus believable agents (*Disco Elysium* ~1M words; *Façade*'s five brittle years) | Agents with memory + reflection + planning (μ=29.89 vs. human 22.95); negotiation grounded in a planner (CICERO) | Fixed premise, stakes, and win conditions (Player-Driven Emergence) |
| Epistemic deduction | Cases hand-crafted to be exactly solvable, then spent in one playthrough (*Obra Dinn*'s 60 fates) | Generates case, clues, and timeline at volume | Solver = verifier: unique-solution check; discard ambiguous cases before the player sees them |
| Living world + its unread epic | Deep simulation (*Dwarf Fortress*, sixteen years) whose history stays locked in data structures | Both halves — the systems *and* a narration engine that reads the simulation out as prose | Simulation invariants; Project Sid's 1,000-agent scale |

Decipherment is the tidiest case because the same evidence appears in both prior papers. *Heaven's Vault* (inkle, 2019) built an entire logographic "Ancient" language — some forty-six glyphs compounding into roughly three thousand translatable words — and made the player's act of translation the core verb, "the Guitar Hero of linguistics" (inkle, 2019). Paper 1 lists it as pioneer of a nearly empty space; Paper 2 names constructing a language as "the most striking fit" for the model, "structured symbol manipulation over a closed system" that is "checkable for internal consistency." Both describe the same object — a formal symbol system — disagreeing only about whether a human or a machine builds it. Real language invention is a technical discipline (Peterson, 2015), and a discipline is exactly what a model executes well under a grammar. Philologists already treat these constructed scripts as genuine decipherment artefacts (CREWS Project, 2019); the model can now supply the artefact.

Social negotiation is the space that changed most between the two papers being written. Paper 1 treats *conversation* as the medium's "most theorized yet least productized mechanic" (Short, 2019), its pioneers cautionary: *Façade* (Mateas & Stern, 2005) consumed five self-funded years and stayed brittle under free text, and *Versu* (Short & Evans, 2013) was simply switched off. The missing ingredient was an agent that could hold a goal, remember, and negotiate — and that ingredient now exists and is measured. Park et al.'s (2023) generative agents — a language model wrapped in a memory stream, retrieval, reflection, and planning — produced emergent social coordination (from one seeded desire, twenty-five agents autonomously spread party invitations, formed acquaintances, and arranged dates over two in-game days) that human raters scored at TrueSkill μ=29.89, above the human-crowdworker baseline of μ=22.95. CICERO closed the other half, reaching roughly the top 10% of human Diplomacy players — more than double the average human score — by grounding its dialogue in a planner so it did not "lie itself into a corner" (Meta FAIR, 2022). Negotiation with memory and hidden goals is no longer a research aspiration; it is a benchmarked capability waiting for a game to house it.

The epistemic detective space carries Paper 1's sharpest unsolved problem, and the model carries the exact discipline that solves it. *Return of the Obra Dinn* (Pope, 2018), reconstructing the fates of sixty souls, and *Outer Wilds* (Beachum, 2019), where knowledge is "the only real thing of value," both suffer the one-shot problem: knowledge, once known, cannot be un-known, so the core loop is spent the instant the player succeeds, and the game is "ruined by one playthrough." Paper 2's generate-then-verify discipline answers this directly. A mystery case — culprit, means, timeline, clue set — is generable at volume; a solver that certifies exactly one deducible solution is a verifier, the same A\*-solvability or uniqueness check that makes MarioGPT and competitive-programming pipelines reliable (Sudhakaran et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022). Ambiguous or unsolvable cases are discarded by construction before a player sees them — which also neutralizes the model's worst failure mode, the confidently-wrong output (Zheng et al., 2025), because an unsound case fails the checker mechanically. A correct-by-construction case that *regenerates* answers the replay ceiling that capped the space's economics.

The living-world space has two halves, and both are the model's. The simulation half — the deep systems of *Dwarf Fortress* and *RimWorld*, designed "as a story generator, not a game" (Sylvester, 2017) — is code and interacting rules, and the field has already scaled Park-style agents from twenty-five to a thousand in Project Sid's Minecraft civilization, observing emergent currency, a spreading religion, and a voted constitution (Altera, 2024). The second half is the one these games have always failed to deliver: *Dwarf Fortress* generates staggering quantities of history that almost no one reads, because it lives in data structures and ASCII. The missing component is a narration engine — a reader — that turns the simulation's events into saga, which is high-volume reactive text over a world model, the model's home ground (Nasir et al., 2024). A living world is a simulation plus its unread epic; a language model is the one tool that supplies both the systems and the narrator.

## Three Games in the Overlap

To make the overlap a design proposal rather than an observation, consider three concepts, each planted where a Paper 1 barrier meets a Paper 2 capability, and each named with the specific evidence that makes it buildable now.

**A dead language that will not stay deciphered.** *Heaven's Vault* built its language once, by hand, and paid for it: the translation content is bespoke reactivity that does not tile, and a third-party estimate puts the flagship's Steam gross near $390,000 — the ceiling the one-shot problem imposes on a game whose core verb is spent once the language is learned. The overlap concept regenerates the language. A generator builds a fresh logographic tongue each playthrough — a phonological inventory, a morphology, a writing system in the discipline Peterson (2015) documents — while a grammar and consistency checker guarantee it is internally solvable, exactly the closed formal system Paper 2 identifies as checkable. The player excavates and translates as before, but no walkthrough can spoil the puzzle, because the walkthrough describes a language that no longer exists. Paper 1's barrier (one-shot exhaustion) is dissolved by Paper 2's discipline (generate-then-verify), and the space's economics change with it.

**A small court.** Take Paper 1's social-negotiation space and give it the architecture *Façade* lacked. A handful of agents — six or eight, comfortably inside Smallville's twenty-five — each carry a persistent memory stream, a private agenda, and evolving relationships, and the player is one courtier among them, winning or losing by conversation rather than combat. The believability is not hoped for but measured, and the ablation ladder tells the designer where to spend: full architecture μ=29.89, drop reflection to 26.88, drop planning too to 25.64, strip memory and it falls to 21.21, below the human baseline (Park et al., 2023). Memory, reflection, and planning — not raw model scale — produce the believability, with an effect size of d=8.16 separating the full agent from the memoryless one. Negotiation with concealed intent is CICERO's demonstrated competence (Meta FAIR, 2022). The one non-negotiable is the cage: a fixed court, fixed stakes, fixed win conditions, because the value of free LLM interaction was found to come precisely from a *fixed authored frame* around it (Peng et al., 2024).

**A case that rewrites itself.** The regenerating mystery combines the epistemic space's purest constraint — knowledge as the only axis of progression — with the generator-plus-verifier loop. Each playthrough, the system composes a fresh case and proves, by solver, that it admits one and only one sound chain of deduction; unsound or ambiguous cases are rejected before generation completes, so what reaches the player is correct by construction. *Obra Dinn*'s sixty-fate deduction, or *Outer Wilds*'s knowledge economy, becomes a renewable resource rather than a single-use one. This is the least speculative of the three, because it is the most mechanical: a mystery with crisp rules admits a solver, a solver is a verifier, and a verifier is exactly what converts the model's fluent, high-volume, sometimes-wrong generation into trustworthy output (Zheng et al., 2025; Sudhakaran et al., 2023).

None of the three asks the model to invent the game. Each asks it to fill a bounded, checkable volume inside a human-authored system — which is the only division of labor the evidence supports, and the subject of the next section.

## The Honest Tension: Homogenization, Unbounded Failure, and the Cage

A synthesis that only celebrated the overlap would be dishonest, because the same literature that makes it real also documents two ways the naive version fails. The first is that generative content regresses toward a bland mean, and it does so by two independent mechanisms — a point worth stating plainly, because most discussions know only one. The familiar mechanism is recursive *model collapse*: when models train on their predecessors' output, the tails of the original distribution vanish first and output converges toward a low-variance mode, a degenerative process Shumailov et al. (2024) show to be irreversible across VAEs, Gaussian mixtures, and LLMs alike (see also Shumailov et al., 2023). The less familiar mechanism needs no recursion at all: alignment itself homogenizes. RLHF's KL-regularized preference optimization, plus a typicality bias in human preference data, penalizes the long tail and converges on safe, central outputs — a measurable drop in diversity in a single generation (Kirk et al., 2023). Both forces erase exactly the rare, surprising output that makes content memorable. The long tail is where the interesting content lives, and two different pressures pull on it at once.

The second failure is structural, and AI Dungeon is its documented case. Unbounded LLM generation has no authored constraint and no stakes: without heavy prompting the model drifts, forgets characters, and contradicts itself, and because nothing is at stake, nothing carries consequence (Latitude, 2019). Worse, it has no floor — after a model upgrade, roughly 31% of AI Dungeon's content was found to be pornographic, some of it illegal, and the crude filters that followed read to users as an "AI lobotomy" (Utah Business, 2021; The Register, 2021). The exploit that defeated those filters is a permanent lesson: players smuggled banned content past moderation through the *token-versus-string gap*, the filter seeing character strings while the model saw tokens. Unbounded generation is not merely unreliable; it is structurally unsafe and structurally stakeless, and no amount of fluency repairs either defect.

The resolution is not to abandon generation but to cage it, and the mixed-initiative literature has held this position for fifteen years. Tanagra (Smith, Whitehead & Mateas, 2010) is the template: the designer places exact geometry and shapes pacing as *constraints*, and a continuously running generator either fills the rest with *guaranteed-playable* level or reports that no level satisfies the constraints. The generator supplies volume; the authored constraint supplies validity and intent. Compton and Mateas's (2015) "casual creators" generalize the stance — automation under human steering, exploring a possibility space — and Tracery (Compton, Kybartas & Mateas, 2015) shows its text form: a hand-written grammar defines the entire generative space, and the machine produces unlimited on-brand output with no fail state. The Player-Driven Emergence study closes the loop empirically: emergent content from free GPT-4 NPCs was valuable, but the value came *from the fixed authored frame around the free interaction* (Peng et al., 2024).

This is where the synthesis earns its deepest connection. Paper 2's central finding was that the model's reliability comes not from intelligence but from pairing generation with a deterministic *verifier* — an A\* check, a grammar, an execution test. The mixed-initiative *cage* is that same discipline scaled up from the level to the whole game: the authored system, constraint, voice, and taste are the oracle for coherence and meaning, the way A\* is the oracle for playability. Verifier and cage are one idea at two altitudes. The win condition is therefore not "the model writes the game" but a division of labor the evidence draws sharply — human-authored system, constraint, voice, and taste; machine-filled volume within the cage — because that is the only configuration in which the model's strength (volume) is admitted and its two failure modes (homogenization, stakelessness) are excluded by construction.

## Discussion

Several findings here are non-obvious enough to restate, because each says something the two prior papers could not say alone. The first and largest has been this paper's spine: the frontier and the tool are the same shape because they are the same fact — not a metaphor but an identity. The set of games too expensive to author and the set a model can cheaply generate are one set described twice, which is precisely why two independent maps, drawn by two papers that did not know of each other's axis, registered line for line. That registration is the strongest evidence that the axis is real.

The second finding is the inversion at the heart of the social space: generative agents were rated *more believable than humans*. The full architecture's μ=29.89 beat the human-crowdworker baseline's μ=22.95, and the clean ablation ladder — 29.89 to 26.88 to 25.64 to 21.21 as memory, reflection, and planning are removed — is causal evidence that structured cognition, not raw scale, produces believability (Park et al., 2023). For a designer this reframes the social-negotiation space entirely: the bottleneck was never whether an agent could be convincing, but whether it could be housed in a game with stakes.

The third finding is that two *independent* homogenizing forces, not one, push generated content toward blandness — recursive model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2024) and alignment-driven mode collapse, which needs no recursion at all (Kirk et al., 2023). A studio guarding only against training-loop collapse will still watch a well-behaved model erase its long tail. This doubles the case for authored constraint: the taste that selects the rare output is not a nicety but the countervailing pressure against two homogenizing gradients at once.

The fourth is a matter of tempo. The social-simulation frontier is not a distant prospect; the field moved from Park's twenty-five agents to Project Sid's thousand-agent civilization — with emergent markets, a spreading religion, and a voted constitution — inside roughly a year (Altera, 2024). The design questions (what cage, what stakes, what taste) are already the binding constraint, ahead of the technology.

The through-line is the one both prior papers reached for and neither could complete. Paper 1 called its diagnosis "hopeful" because a frontier gated by cost is one that falling costs can open; Paper 2 named the mechanism that lowers the cost but stopped at capability. What they jointly imply is that the tool changes the economics of a specific, previously uneconomical region of design — and that region happens to be the medium's most important unexplored frontier.

### Limitations

This argument inherits the limits of its evidence. The believability and negotiation results are laboratory findings — TrueSkill rankings of sandbox agents (Park et al., 2023) and anonymous online Diplomacy (Meta FAIR, 2022) — not shipped, sustained games, and the gap between a benchmarked capability and a durable product is exactly the gap *Façade* and *Versu* fell into. Several figures are secondary reporting: the word counts are studio and press statements, *Heaven's Vault*'s ~$390,000 gross is a third-party tracker estimate, and the AI Dungeon percentages are journalistic accounts of an internal finding. The benchmark scores are snapshots of a fast-moving target (METR, 2025), and some will date. Finally, the axis this paper unifies is real but not total: the frontier's two kinesthetic spaces (impossible space, time-as-material) sit outside the model's reach, and this synthesis makes no claim there — it argues that where the frontier is made of text and systems, which is most of it, the tool and the territory coincide.

## Conclusion

The two prior papers in this set were written toward different questions and arrived at one place. The first asked why the videogame's richest design spaces stayed empty and answered that they are built from hand-authored reactivity and deep systems — content that does not tile — fenced by one-shot exhaustion, artisanal development, and, in one case, a patent good until 2036. The second asked which games a language model could genuinely build and answered that its advantage concentrates in code, rules, text, and verifiable generated content and vanishes in bespoke art and hand-tuned feel. These are not two findings but one: a game's material composition is a single property read twice — once by the cost of paying humans to author it, once by the cost of asking a machine to generate it — and the frontier is simply the set of games where the first cost was prohibitive and the second is small.

That the two maps register so exactly, down to the shared exclusion of the feel-based edges, is the paper's central evidence: the medium's most important unexplored region and the language model's sharpest strength are one territory seen from two sides. The overlap is already demonstrable — a conlang the machine can build and regenerate, agents more believable than humans negotiating with hidden goals, mysteries correct by construction, simulations at last supplied with their narrator — and the honest tension is equally clear: unbounded generation homogenizes by two independent mechanisms and fails without stakes, so the win condition is a mixed-initiative cage, authored constraint around machine-filled volume, the verifier of Paper 2 scaled to the whole game. The tool and the frontier were waiting for each other. But a dissolved budget removes the obstacle, not the need — the authored system, the constraint, the voice, and above all the taste that selects the one output worth keeping from the flood of plausible ones remain the human's to supply. What the machine changes is the economics of a region the medium could never before afford to explore. What it cannot change is that someone still has to know what is worth exploring there.
