# Three Research Papers: The Under-Explored Game Frontier and the Machine That Fits It

This directory holds a three-paper research set. It is the rigorous, sourced
companion to the three shorter essays in [`../`](../) — same three questions,
but argued from evidence, with inline citations and real references rather than
from reflection alone.

The three papers were built the same way: five parallel web-research passes
gathered real, citable sources (design theory, ~25 pioneer games, code-LLM
benchmarks, game-development economics, and the AI-content-in-games literature);
the papers were drafted from those briefs; and every load-bearing figure, quote,
award, and arXiv ID was then re-checked by an independent adversarial fact-check
pass, with corrections applied. Citations are drawn only from sources the
research actually surfaced. (One methodological limit worth stating plainly:
direct page-fetching was blocked by the environment's egress policy, so the
research rested on web-search retrieval of real pages rather than full-text
reads; the fact-check pass re-confirmed the load-bearing claims against that
same channel.)

## The throughline

All three papers turn on one idea, stated most fully in the third: a game's
**material composition** — whether it is made of *reactive text and deep
systems* or of *bespoke art and hand-tuned feel* — is a single property that
gets read twice. Read once by the economics of paying humans to author a game,
it explains why the medium's richest design spaces stayed empty: reactive
content **"does not tile."** Read again by the economics of asking a language
model to generate a game, it explains where that model's comparative advantage
lies. The frontier and the tool turn out to be the same shape because they are
the same fact seen from two sides.

## The papers

1. **[The Wrong Axis](01-unexplored-game-spaces-research.md)** — *Verbs,
   Computational Form, and the Under-Explored Design Spaces of the Videogame.*
   Argues that marketing genres classify the wrong axis (Apperley, 2006) and
   that a real *design space* is fixed by a game's verbs, subject matter, and
   computational form. Re-sorted along that axis the commercial medium is a
   two-verb monoculture — violence and acquisition — which content analysis,
   Koster's mastery theory of fun, and even platform-moderation policy each
   independently predict. Surveys seven under-explored spaces through their
   pioneer titles and argues the frontier stayed closed for economic, technical,
   and even legal reasons, not for want of imagination. *~4,400 words.*

2. **[A Capability Analysis](02-ai-game-programming-research.md)** — *Video
   Games an AI Language Model Would Excel at Programming.* An evidence-based
   self-assessment. Reads the code-generation benchmarks (HumanEval saturation
   versus LiveCodeBench Pro's 0%-on-hard cliff; package hallucination; the METR
   horizon) against game-development economics (art and feel as the dominant
   AAA cost). The two datasets converge on one principle — reliability comes
   from pairing generation with a deterministic *verifier* — and locate the
   model's advantage in games made of code, rules, text, and *checkable*
   generated content. *~3,950 words.*

3. **[Content That Does Not Tile](03-intersection-research.md)** — *The
   Under-Explored Game Frontier and a Language Model's Comparative Advantage as
   a Single Axis.* The synthesis. Shows the frontier of Paper 1 and the
   advantage of Paper 2 are one territory seen from two sides — the exclusions
   align as tightly as the inclusions — and grounds the overlap in current
   evidence (generative agents rated more believable than humans; CICERO's
   negotiation; the ~800k → ~1M → ~2M-word reactive-narrative cost curve).
   Proposes three buildable game concepts, then confronts the honest tension:
   generative content homogenizes by two independent mechanisms and fails
   without stakes, so the win condition is a mixed-initiative *cage* —
   human-authored constraint, voice, and taste around machine-filled volume.
   *~4,790 words.*

## Reading order

Read them in numbered order. Paper 3 quotes the closing arguments of Papers 1
and 2 directly and builds on their shared economic diagnosis; it will lose much
of its force read alone.
