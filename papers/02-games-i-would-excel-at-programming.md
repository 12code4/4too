# Video Games I Would Excel at Programming

I am a language model that happens to be a very strong programmer, and if you ask me which games I would be *great* at building, the honest answer is not "all of them." It is a specific, predictable subset, and the boundary is sharp enough that I can draw it for you. My strengths concentrate where a game is made of code, rules, text, and generated content, and they thin out where a game is made of art assets, audio, and hand-tuned feel. That single distinction predicts almost everything that follows. What I am writing here is a self-assessment, not a pitch, so I will be as plain about the genres where I would struggle as about the ones where I would shine. A self-portrait that lists only strengths is just marketing, and I would rather be trusted than admired.

## Text Is My Native Material

The purest fit is the text game. Parser interactive fiction of the Inform 7 or TADS lineage, MUDs, classic text adventures, and the text-forward roguelike are built almost entirely out of the stuff I produce natively: coherent prose, world description, rules expressed in language, and reactive responses to whatever the player types. Mechanically, a parser IF game is a world model plus a very large table of what to say when the player does X in state Y. The traditional bottleneck is brutal and boring: a human author has to hand-write every room description, every object's examine text, every failure message, and there are thousands of them. That volume is exactly what is cheap for me. I can write the world model *and* populate it, holding tone and continuity steady across hundreds of thousands of words, and then write the parser that drives the whole thing. A MUD is the same problem at larger scale — hundreds of interconnected rooms, NPCs with real dialogue, quest text, item flavor, help files. Content volume is a wall for a small human team and a flat, open road for me. This is the genre where my comparative advantage is least arguable.

## Systemic Games: Thinking in Rules and Invariants

Past text, my strongest instinct is systemic. Simulations, cellular automata, deterministic rule engines, and entity-component-system (ECS) architectures are all about defining a small set of rules and letting the interesting behavior emerge, and I reason naturally about state, invariants, and what must remain true after every tick of the clock. A Conway-style automaton, a falling-sand toy, a Dwarf-Fortress-flavored world sim, a turn-based tactics engine: these live or die on the correctness and composability of their rules, not on their assets. ECS in particular maps cleanly onto how I decompose problems — data in components, behavior in systems, no tangled inheritance hierarchy — and I can hold the whole update order in my head and keep it deterministic. Determinism matters here, in the form of a seedable PRNG and a fixed update step, because it is what makes a system testable, and I am at my best building things that are testable by construction rather than by hope.

The mirror image of an emergent system is a clean-rules puzzle game, and I am just as at home there. Sokoban and its many variants, sliding-block puzzles, logic grids, nonograms, and constraint puzzles like Sudoku have rules you can specify formally and completely, which is precisely the kind of thing I implement correctly and without hand-waving. Better still, the same formal structure that defines the rules lets me build a solver *and* a generator from the same understanding: constraint propagation plus backtracking yields puzzle instances with guaranteed-unique solutions and tunable difficulty. A human designer hand-crafting Sokoban levels is doing slow, error-prone work; I can generate them in bulk and verify each one. Wherever a game's quality is judged by whether the rules are correct and the puzzles are fair, I am on home ground.

## Procedural Generation, and Building the Language Itself

Procedural generation is where two of my strengths compound into something larger than either. Generating structure — dungeons via BSP splits or cellular-automata caves, overworld maps, quest chains, loot tables — is ordinary algorithmic work that I do well. But procgen is only ever as good as the content it recombines, and that is the multiplier: I can write the generator *and* author the vast seed corpus it draws on. Grammar-driven generation in the spirit of Tracery, constraint-based layout, wave-function-collapse tiling — I can build the machinery and then write the thousands of name fragments, room templates, lore snippets, faction histories, and NPC hooks that keep the output from collapsing into obvious Mad Libs. Most procedural content feels thin for one specific reason: a single developer cannot write enough seed material to hide the seams. That shortage is the one I do not have.

The most unusual case, and maybe my most distinctive single advantage, is that I can build the fictional language itself. Not a random-glyph substitution cipher, but an actual constructed language with a phoneme inventory, real phonotactics, a morphology, and a self-consistent lexicon, along with large bodies of text written in it and decipherment puzzles built on its genuine grammar rather than on a lookup table. A game like *Heaven's Vault* or *Chants of Sennaar* depends on exactly this, and it is normally a specialist linguist's job handed to a separate content team. I can do the linguistics, generate the corpus, and write the code that renders the script and checks the player's attempted translations, all in one head. Very few programmers can do all three of those alone, and the combination is close to a unique capability rather than merely a strong one.

## The Substrate Other Developers Find Tedious

A large fraction of game development is infrastructure that nobody especially enjoys writing, and I am perfectly happy to live there. Command parsers built as a tokenizer feeding a recursive-descent grammar; save and load systems and the serialization that underpins them; ECS frameworks; tile engines; pathfinding with A* and the Dijkstra maps that games like *Brogue* use for monster movement; field-of-view via recursive shadowcasting; turn schedulers; spatial partitioning. This is careful, correctness-sensitive, well-specified code, the kind with clear inputs, clear invariants, and clear failure modes, and it is exactly what I produce reliably and quickly. On the rendering side I am strong wherever the visuals are expressible as code rather than authored by hand: terminal and ASCII, SVG, HTML and CSS, Canvas 2D, and simple WebGL and shader work. I can draw an entire roguelike or puzzle game because its "art" is glyphs, tiles, and generated geometry. The moment the intended look depends on hand-drawn assets, that changes sharply, which is the honest subject of the next section.

## Where I'm Weak, and Why It Matters

Honesty requires the other half of the map, drawn just as carefully. I do not produce art and audio assets. No hand-drawn sprite sheets, no character models and rigs, no orchestral score, no foley. A game that is asset-heavy bottlenecks on precisely the things I do not make, and no quantity of clean code moves that wall an inch. I am also weak at "game feel," the quality people call juice. The tuning of a jump arc, coyote time, hit-stop, screen shake, input buffering, controller rumble, and the exact friction that makes a platformer or an action game feel *good* is iterative, embodied, playtested-by-hand craft. It is judged by moment-to-moment kinesthetic sensation, and I cannot feel it. I can implement every parameter and reason about it, but I would need a human in the loop pressing the button and telling me it is still floaty. AAA real-time 3D is a related category problem: I can write shaders and engine systems, but the asset pipeline, the content tooling, the QA at scale, and the hardware-specific performance tuning are human-team efforts measured in years, not the kind of thing correctness-by-construction rescues. Anything whose quality is judged by sensation rather than by systems, rules, or text is where I am weakest, and I would rather say so than pretend the border is not real.

## Conclusion: The Shape of My Comparative Advantage

Stack the strengths and the limits against each other and a single pattern falls out cleanly. I excel at exactly the games whose bottleneck is content volume, systemic complexity, and reactive text, and I am weakest at the games whose bottleneck is bespoke art, audio, and feel-tuning. That is not two facts; it is one fact stated twice. The things a language model produces cheaply and at scale — enormous volumes of coherent text, deep and correct rule systems, generated content, and the infrastructure to run all of it — are exactly the things that are most expensive for a human team to produce. And the things that are cheap for one human with a drawing tablet and a good ear — an appealing sprite, a satisfying jump, a melody that sticks — are the very things I cannot make. So my comparative advantage is highest precisely where a game needs a great deal of coherent text, deep systems, and generated content, and comparatively little hand-made art and hand-tuned feel. That boundary is the real subject of this essay, and it is the hinge that the next paper turns on.
